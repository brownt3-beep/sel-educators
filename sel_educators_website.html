The developmental foundation

Why early experiences matter later

Adolescent social and emotional development does not begin in adolescence. Biology and experience interact over time, shaping stress responses, relationships, and opportunities to practice coping skills.

🤰

Prenatal development & care

Nutrition, prenatal health care, and exposure to sustained stress or harmful substances can influence fetal development. Supportive prenatal care can reduce preventable risks; outcomes are not predetermined by any single experience.

🧸

Early relationships

Responsive caregivers help young children feel safe, practice calming with adult support, and develop trust. Predictable routines, play, and language-rich interactions provide foundations for emotional understanding.

🌿

Adolescent outcomes

Earlier experiences can shape later emotion regulation, peer relationships, and responses to stress. Importantly, caring relationships and skill-building at school and home can continue to support growth during adolescence.

Educator takeaway: Early adversity is a risk factor, not a destiny. Avoid assumptions about individual students or families. Consistent, respectful, culturally responsive support matters at every age. [1, 2]
Three selected developmental stages

Milestones + two classroom strategies per stage

Milestones are approximate patterns, not deadlines. Development varies by individual, disability, language, culture, and context. Adapt activities to student needs.

Ages ~3–5

Early childhood

Typical milestones: Naming basic feelings; beginning to take turns; joining cooperative pretend play; using adult support to handle frustration and conflicts.

Try in class

  • 1. Feelings check-in: Invite children to choose a picture or word for their current feeling; accept passing.
  • 2. Puppet problem-solving: Act out a sharing conflict and practice naming feelings and offering two fair solutions.
Ages ~6–11

Middle childhood

Typical milestones: Growing perspective-taking; deeper friendships; more nuanced emotion vocabulary; increased ability to follow group norms and use coping strategies independently.

Try in class

  • 1. Perspective circles: Read a short scenario and discuss how different people might feel and why.
  • 2. Collaborative challenge: Assign rotating roles in a team task; reflect on listening, disagreement, and repair.
Ages ~12–18

Adolescence

Typical milestones: Exploring identity and values; increasing desire for autonomy; navigating complex peer relationships; developing longer-term decision-making and coping skills.

Try in class

  • 1. Values-to-actions journal: Privately reflect on a personal value and one small action that reflects it; sharing is optional.
  • 2. Scenario rehearsal: In pairs, practice respectful boundary-setting, help-seeking, and conflict repair using fictional situations.

These examples align with broad developmental research and evidence-based SEL principles; they are not diagnostic checklists. [1–3]

A teachable skill

What is self-regulation?

Self-regulation is the developing ability to notice and manage emotions, attention, thoughts, and behavior in ways that support goals and relationships. It does not mean never feeling upset.

What it looks like

  • A preschooler asks an adult for help instead of grabbing a toy.
  • An elementary student takes a short pause before responding to criticism.
  • A teen notices frustration, chooses a coping strategy, and returns to a challenging assignment.

Three ways teachers can model it

1
Think aloud. “I’m frustrated that our plan changed. I’ll pause, then choose our next step.”
2
Co-regulate before correcting. Use a calm voice, offer a choice, and help students reconnect before problem-solving.
3
Practice predictable routines. Teach brief check-ins, movement breaks, and reflection; rehearse when students are calm.
Try this routine: Notice → Name → Choose → Reflect. “What am I feeling? What might help? How did that choice work?” Offer different tools rather than requiring every child to use the same technique. [2, 3]
School + home partnership

Simple SEL practices families can use

Share these ideas as invitations, not requirements. Families can adapt them to their language, culture, schedule, and child's preferences.

Early childhood

Feelings in everyday moments

Name emotions during books or play (“The character looks disappointed”). Offer two calming choices such as drawing or sitting together. Practice taking turns during a short game.

Middle childhood

Problem-solving together

At a relaxed time, ask “What happened? What could you try next?” Practice a family pause-and-reset routine and notice effort, kindness, and repair rather than only outcomes.

Adolescence

Autonomy with connection

Invite teens to choose when and how to talk. Listen without immediately fixing; collaborate on sleep-friendly routines, stress-management options, and plans for seeking support from trusted adults.

Conversation starter for all ages: “What was one feeling you noticed today, and what helped you through it?” It is okay if a child prefers not to answer.

Implementation

Make SEL part of the day

Start of class · 2 minutesOptional emotional check-in or quiet arrival routine.
During learning · 5 minutesPractice a cooperative or perspective-taking activity.
Closing · 2 minutesReflect on a strategy that helped, without grading emotions.

SEL works best when embedded in a supportive classroom climate and taught explicitly over time, rather than used only after conflict. [3]
